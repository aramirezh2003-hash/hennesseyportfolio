E-commerce · Product discovery and purchase

KitLab FS

Make a 1,200-product catalogue easy to trust, then make the purchase easier to finish.

Case study

01 · Context 02 · Problem 03 · User flow 04 · Thinking 05 · Solution 06 · Results

01 / Context

What I was trying to change

KitLabFS is an online store for club, national-team and retro football jerseys, with 1,200+ products and 7,822 variants. I designed and launched it, owning product pages, funnels, checkout and inventory flows. The store drew 450,000+ visitors in its first three months and grew to 550+ orders.

02 / Problem

The interface was only the symptom.

Choices such as player versions, patches, sizing and name customization were hard to understand. Shoppers also dropped off between the cart and the completed purchase.

The questionHow might we help customers move from thousands of jerseys to a confident purchase, without making any step feel overloaded?

03 / User flow

Click through the flow

Shopper flow for the live store. Click a step to see what happens next.

Start or end User action Decision Choice System

Shopper

Thousands of jerseys across clubs, nations, competitions and retro. Discovery is the bottleneck, so the catalogue is organized the way fans shop.

Each product page shows the choices that matter for that shirt, in order.

Categories follow how fans browse, not how the inventory is stored.

Player versions, patches and sizing are revealed in a guided sequence, so no step feels overloaded.

Every customization updates the preview and the price immediately, so the shopper always knows what they are buying.

Selections stay on screen so nothing is lost between the product page and checkout.

Cart, checkout and completed-sale rates are tracked at each stage to decide what to design next. Result: 3.8% completed sale rate.

04 / My thinking

The path from signal to decision

01

Observe

Funnel data showed where shoppers left: 23.7% reached the cart, 10.4% reached checkout and 3.8% completed a sale.

02

Frame

Discovery and the purchase path mattered more than visual polish.

03

Explore

Organize the catalogue around how fans browse: club, country, competition, season and type.

04

Decide

Reveal customization in a guided sequence, and keep selections and price visible.

05

Refine

Review builds with developers and fix confusing interactions before and after release.

05 / Solution

The design bet

Sequence the journey instead of exposing every choice at once.

Case study

01 · Context 02 · Problem 03 · User flow 04 · Thinking 05 · Solution 06 · Results

01 / Context

What I was trying to change

MatchUp is a mobile concept for creating local soccer matches, selecting available positions and sharing field-booking costs. It connects organizers who have booked a field with players looking for a local game.

02 / Problem

The interface was only the symptom.

Players rely on scattered group chats with incomplete information about cost, skill level and open positions. Organizers manually build teams and repeatedly chase payments.

The questionHow might we create one trusted experience for forming teams, selecting positions and sharing field costs, for two different users at once?

03 / User flow

Click through the flow

Concept flow. Organizers and players move through the same match, from different starting points.

Start or end User action Decision Choice System

Organizer

Organizers no longer rebuild teams or chase payments by hand.

Players see availability and cost before they commit.

Player

Skill level, location and cost are visible before anyone commits.

Payment is shown with the match, so no one chases anyone.

One shared match state from discovery to kickoff.

04 / My thinking

The path from signal to decision

01

Observe

Trust was fragmented across messages, people and payment requests.

02

Frame

Map organizer and player needs separately, then connect the moments that must stay in sync.

03

Explore

Make openings, skill level, location and cost visible before anyone commits.

04

Decide

Let players choose a position and pay in the context of the match.

05

Refine

Carry certainty from discovery through kickoff and post-match review.

05 / Solution

The design bet

Give organizers and players one shared match state.

Case study

01 · Context 02 · Problem 03 · User flow 04 · Thinking 05 · Solution 06 · Results

01 / Context

What I was trying to change

CommerceFlow is a concept for an operations dashboard that brings sales, orders, inventory, customers and advertising data together, so multi-store operators can spot urgent issues and act without switching between tools.

02 / Problem

The interface was only the symptom.

Operators repeatedly switch between storefronts, advertising platforms, spreadsheets and supplier systems just to understand performance and manage daily operations.

The questionHow might we help operators see what changed, understand why, and move directly to the right action?

03 / User flow

Click through the flow

Concept flow for an operator checking the overview each day.

Start or end User action Decision Choice System

Operator

The overview answers 'what changed?' before showing everything available.

Each alert points to sales, inventory or advertising, the place where the answer lives.

04 / My thinking

The path from signal to decision

01

Observe

Context was expensive because the story lived across tools and tabs.

02

Frame

The overview should answer “what changed?” before showing everything available.

03

Explore

Pair every important signal with the module that can explain it.

04

Decide

Make alerts lead toward resolution, not into yet another inbox.

05

Refine

Use a component system that can grow with teams, stores and roles.

05 / Solution
