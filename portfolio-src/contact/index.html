01 / Context

What I was trying to change

KitLabFS is an online store for club, national-team and retro football jerseys, with 1,200+ products and 7,822 variants. I designed and launched it, owning product pages, funnels, checkout and inventory flows. The store drew 450,000+ visitors in its first three months and grew to 550+ orders.

02 / Problem

The interface was only the symptom.

Choices such as player versions, patches, sizing and name customization were hard to understand. Shoppers also dropped off between the cart and the completed purchase.

The questionHow might we help customers move from thousands of jerseys to a confident purchase, without making any step feel overloaded?

03 / User flow

Click through the flow

The shopper journey for the live store, in four stages. Click a step to open what happens next, or use Expand all.

Start or end User action Decision Choice System

1 · Find a shirt

Visitors arrive from ads, search or the homepage, so every entry point should land on something shoppable.

The menu groups clubs by confederation (UEFA, CONMEBOL, CONCACAF, AFC, CAF, OFC), so a shopper reaches a league in two clicks. On mobile the same menu opens in place, level by level.

The page opens with the choice already applied as a removable chip, so the shopper never starts from zero.

Results sit in the same grid, with the same filters, so refining feels familiar.

Badges such as Trending, Bestseller, New and Retro help a shopper decide what to open first.

Category, confederation, country, kit version, season, size, price range and in-stock only. Active filters sit above the results and clear with one tap.

The catalogue holds about 1,200 products and 7,822 variants.

A running count tells the shopper how close they are. Load more shows progress, for example 24 of the total.

The shopper moves to the stage where most decisions are made.

2 · Choose and customize

This page was redesigned after the first version let people play with every option and leave.

Free shipping and an estimated delivery date sit in the buy panel, so shipping is never a surprise at checkout.

The size guide is one tap away, so doubts about fit are solved on the page.

Jersey, personalization and total are listed in the panel. There are no toggles to flip, so there is nothing to play with and no phantom price changes.

This is the decision point the redesign was built around.

Selections, price and shipping were already decided on the page, so the cart confirms rather than surprises.

Specifications, sizing guide, care and fit, and reviews are one tab away.

3 · Cart and checkout

It opens as a drawer, so the shopper keeps their place in the catalogue.

Progress toward free shipping and the third-shirt offer sits at the top of the cart, where it nudges larger orders.

The offer text changes as the order grows, for example how many more shirts unlock the free one.

Shipping and the total were visible on the product page and in the cart, so the shopper arrives having already decided.

Result: 550+ orders, average order value up from US$43 to US$68 after the purchase redesign, and a 3.8% completed-sale rate.

4 · After the sale

Support questions cost time, so the post-purchase experience answers them before they are asked.

The order number is the key to everything after the sale.

Reachable from the navigation and the footer.

Order number, placed date and estimated delivery appear in a dark header. A five-step bar shows Processing, Packed, Shipped, Out for delivery and Delivered.

The latest scan, for example in transit at a facility, and a timeline of every earlier scan.

The shopper never needed support.

Where is my order? How do I return an item? Package says delivered but I did not receive it. Wrong item received.

Asking for the order number and issue type up front means the support team starts with the facts, not a back-and-forth.

The path from signal to decision

05 / Solution

The design bet

Sequence the journey instead of exposing every choice at once.
Compare
Desktop
Mobile and detail

Drag the handle to compare the first product page with the redesign.

BeforeAfter
What we saw

Shoppers treated the page as a toy. Every switch revealed a price and a box to type in, so people tried their own name, watched the price change and left. Those sessions inflated product views and checkout visits with no intent to buy.

What changed

We compacted the choices into one sequence: version, size, personalization and a visible price breakdown, with no switches to flip. The total is shown before the button.

Shipping up front

Delivery and free shipping now sit in the buy panel. Before, some customers reached checkout, found the shipping cost and left. Now they decide before they get there.

Why it matters

Less playing around means cleaner funnel data. Shoppers who reach checkout have already made the decision, so they finish faster and with fewer second thoughts.

01 / Context

What I was trying to change

MatchUp is a mobile app for creating local soccer matches, selecting available positions and sharing field-booking costs. It connects organizers who have booked a field with players looking for a local game.

02 / Problem

The interface was only the symptom.

Players rely on scattered group chats with incomplete information about cost, skill level and open positions. Organizers manually build teams and repeatedly chase payments.

The questionHow might we create one trusted experience for forming teams, selecting positions and sharing field costs, for two different users at once?

03 / User flow

Click through the flow

Organizers and players move through the same match, from different starting points.

One shared match state from discovery to kickoff.
